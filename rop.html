01 Rules of Procedure Two special formats

AIPPM — How the Meet runs

The All India Political Parties Meet is not a UN committee: there are no countries, no flags on the placard, and no resolutions. You portray a real Indian political leader, argue your party's line, and fight for a joint statement everyone can sign.

01Portfolios, not countries

Every delegate represents a leader from an Indian political party — you speak as that person, with their party's positions and rivalries.

02Sessions of deliberation

Debate flows through moderated discussion on sub-topics set by the Moderator, with informal huddles to build cross-party blocs.

03Points & interjections

Sharp, short interjections replace formal points — wit and political knowledge score as heavily as volume.

04Documentation

The Meet works toward a joint communiqué; dissenting blocs may publish their own notes of dissent.

05Press is watching

The International Press sits in — expect your best one-liner (or worst slip) in the evening paper.

06Winning the room

Awards weigh research, consistency of portrayal, and your role in the final consensus — not just airtime.

Scenario test — 6 questions 0 / 6 correct

Q1The Moderator opens a sub-topic your leader's party has never taken a public position on. The room expects you to speak. What is the strongest in-portfolio play?

Q2Consensus is forming around a proposal your leader's party genuinely opposes. Refusing outright isolates you; signing betrays the portfolio. What do you do?

Q3A delegate quotes a statistic that you know is fabricated, and the room is starting to accept it. What's the sharpest response?

Q4Which of these is a genuine procedural break in AIPPM, rather than merely a weak move?

Q5The International Press publishes a report that quotes you accurately but frames your position damagingly. What's the best play?

Q6Two delegates make identical points. One gets the Best Delegate nod. What most likely separated them?

NBA All-Time Greats Draft — How it runs

A semi-crisis unlike anything else on the floor: general managers drafting from every era of basketball history to build the greatest roster of all time — then defending it under fire from the room and the crisis desk.

01You are a GM

Each delegate manages a franchise. Your job: assemble the best possible roster from every player in NBA history.

02The draft

Picks proceed in a snake order set by the Executive Board. Once a player is taken, they're gone — value your board.

03Defend the pick

After each round the floor opens: you justify your pick on eras, stats, fit and legacy — and attack everyone else's.

04Trades & directives

Between rounds, GMs may negotiate trades and send directives to the crisis desk — injuries, scandals and twists arrive without warning.

05The verdict

Rosters are judged by committee vote and Executive Board scoring across balance, argumentation and adaptability.

06Know the game

Stats win arguments, but stories win rooms — eras, rivalries and championship DNA all count.

Scenario test — 6 questions 0 / 6 correct

Q1You hold the last pick of a snake round, which means you also pick first in the next. Two elite guards remain and no elite centre. What's the strongest use of that double pick?

Q2The crisis desk announces a career-ending injury to your best player mid-draft. What's the strongest response?

Q3An opponent argues your 1960s pick is inflated because the league was smaller and less athletic. What's the strongest rebuttal?

Q4What exactly makes this a semi-crisis committee rather than a full crisis?

Q5A rival offers you two good players for your one superstar, and your roster is thin. Spot the flaw in accepting reflexively.

Q6Two GMs finish with statistically comparable rosters. What most likely decides the verdict?

Note — these primers and scenarios are a warm-up written by the Secretariat, not the rulebook. The binding ROP for every committee ships with its background guide before the conference.
